Chapter 1: Introduction

Background and Business Problem(s)

CVRx, Inc. is a commercial-stage medical device company focused on developing, manufacturing, and commercializing neuromodulation therapies for cardiovascular disease. Its primary commercial product, Barostim, uses Baroreflex Activation Therapy to modulate the autonomic nervous system and is intended for eligible patients with heart failure. CVRx’s commercial success depends not only on the clinical value of Barostim but also on the effectiveness of its field organization in supporting physician awareness, patient access, and therapy adoption.

Salesforce is an important customer relationship management platform within CVRx’s commercial operations. The system provides a centralized environment for field users to document customer interactions, maintain account and opportunity information, support sales processes, and provide information that can be used for commercial planning and decision-making. However, the value of a CRM system depends on users entering information accurately, consistently, and at the appropriate time.

CVRx has identified inconsistent Salesforce adoption and usage among members of its field organization as a business problem. Differences in record completeness, update timeliness, workflow consistency, and other usage behaviors may reduce data quality and limit the organization’s ability to rely on Salesforce information for commercial decision-making. At the same time, differences in adoption may reflect underlying factors such as perceived usefulness, ease of use, workflow compatibility, training, managerial support, trust in Salesforce data, or administrative burden.

This project will evaluate current Salesforce adoption, identify factors associated with stronger or weaker adoption, segment users based on meaningful usage patterns, and develop targeted engagement strategies based on the resulting evidence. The project will also evaluate whether a targeted engagement intervention improves meaningful Salesforce adoption, data quality and timeliness, and user perceptions. The broader goal is to provide CVRx with an evidence-based approach for improving Salesforce adoption and strengthening the usefulness of CRM data within its commercial field organization.

Project Objectives

The project objectives describe what CVRx wants to accomplish with the team, rather than a statistical technique. Based on the faculty project briefing and kickoff discussion with Professor Moore, the four objectives are:

  1. Measure current Salesforce adoption. Establish a baseline for meaningful use and data reliability across approved field workflows.
  2. Identify adoption barriers and drivers. Understand what helps or prevents field users from keeping Salesforce information current and useful.
  3. Segment users and develop a targeted engagement strategy. Group users by relevant patterns and design support or communications that address their needs.
  4. Implement and evaluate an engagement campaign. Pilot a limited intervention and assess changes in use, data quality, and user feedback.

Analytics Objectives

AO1 — Measure Meaningful Salesforce Adoption (Luis Berumen)

My analytical objective is to develop a Salesforce Adoption Index and use it to examine how adoption differs across approved roles, territories, and time periods. The index would bring together opportunity update timeliness, record completeness, documentation of customer interactions, consistency with defined workflows, and a smaller measure of platform activity. I would first confirm with CVRx which fields and actions are actually required for its commercial decisions. Then I would use approved Salesforce records to calculate separate scores for each area and look at the overall pattern. The unit of analysis could be a deidentified user-month, with results shared in aggregate.

This objective answers PO1 by showing where use is consistent and where records are late or incomplete. It could help CVRx decide which workflow needs attention first and provide a baseline for evaluating the later pilot. I would compare like with like: a user with few active opportunities should not receive a low score simply because there was less work to enter. Research supports matching usage measures to the task rather than relying on time spent or access alone (burtonjones2006?). Sales technology can also affect work in different ways, so an overall score should be accompanied by the separate measures and context (Ahearne et al. 2008). This is a proposed measure; its final rules and weights depend on the data CVRx approves and the field team’s actual workflow.

AO2 – Identify Drivers and Barriers of Meaningful Salesforce Adoption

Lead: Mickyas Shawel

Identify and quantify the factors associated with meaningful Salesforce adoption among CVRx sales representatives, with particular attention to the behavioral, organizational, and user-experience factors that may encourage or inhibit consistent CRM use.

This analytics objective builds on AO1 by moving beyond the measurement of Salesforce adoption to investigate why adoption levels differ across users. The analysis will examine available Salesforce usage measures alongside relevant employee, workflow, training, and engagement variables to identify factors associated with stronger or weaker adoption. Potential drivers may include perceived usefulness, ease of use, managerial support, training effectiveness, workflow compatibility, and the perceived value of CRM information, while barriers may include administrative burden, system complexity, redundant processes, insufficient training, or limited integration with established sales routines.

The analysis will use the variables available in the final dataset to evaluate relationships between potential adoption factors and the meaningful-adoption measures established in AO1. Depending on the measurement structure and data quality, appropriate methods may include descriptive comparisons, correlation analysis, group comparisons, and multivariable regression or classification models. The purpose is not only to determine whether a factor is associated with adoption, but also to estimate the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of those relationships while accounting for other relevant variables.

The findings from AO2 will provide an evidence-based explanation of the factors most closely associated with Salesforce adoption and will directly support subsequent analytics objectives. In particular, the identified drivers and barriers can help inform the user segments developed in AO3 and provide a stronger analytical basis for designing targeted engagement strategies rather than applying the same intervention to all Salesforce users.

AO3 — Salesforce User Segmentation and Targeted Engagement

Lead: Tim Arana

PO3: Segment users and develop a targeted engagement strategy. Group users by relevant patterns and design support or communications that address their needs.

AO3: Analyze Salesforce usage patterns to identify distinct user segments based on adoption behavior and determine how those segments can inform targeted engagement strategies.

Data Source: Approved CVRx Salesforce user-level usage and activity data, including relevant adoption measures developed in AO1. Where available and approved, AO2 survey measures describing adoption barriers, drivers, needs, and relevant user or organizational characteristics may also be used to profile and interpret the resulting segments.

How to Address AO3

AO3 will examine differences in Salesforce adoption behavior among CVRx users to determine whether meaningful user segments can be identified. Relevant behavioral measures may include activity frequency, update timeliness, record completeness, workflow consistency, and other approved adoption indicators developed through AO1. Depending on the available sample size and data quality, candidate segments may be developed using a transparent rule-based approach or empirical clustering. Findings from AO2 may then be used to profile and interpret the segments based on relevant adoption barriers, drivers, needs, and contextual characteristics.

How AO3 Will Help Achieve PO3

AO3 directly supports PO3 by identifying whether CVRx Salesforce users can be organized into meaningful groups with different adoption patterns and needs. Rather than applying the same engagement strategy to all users, the resulting segments can provide an analytical basis for developing more targeted communications, training, support, or other engagement strategies. These segments can also inform the targeted intervention that will subsequently be implemented and evaluated under AO4.

AO4 — Implement and evaluate an engagement campaign.

Lead: Wenting Jiang

This analytics objective is to evaluate the effectiveness of a targeted field engagement intervention designed to improve meaningful Salesforce adoption among CVRx’s commercial field users. The intervention may include role-specific communications, short workflow demonstrations, training sessions, manager toolkits, or other engagement tactics selected based on the adoption barriers and user segments identified in the earlier objectives. The evaluation will compare meaningful Salesforce adoption, data quality, workflow consistency, and user perceptions before and after implementation. When operationally feasible, the analysis will also compare the intervention group with a reference group.

This objective supports PO4 by implementing an engagement strategy, measuring its outcomes, and identifying what CVRx should continue, modify, or expand. Evaluation measures will be aligned with employees’ actual work requirements rather than relying only on login frequency or time spent in the platform(Rodriguez and Boyer 2020). Because sales technology can affect employees differently depending on their roles and working conditions, the results will also be interpreted in the context of the selected user segment and intervention design (Abdillah 2025). The findings will help CVRx determine whether the campaign produces meaningful and measurable improvements in Salesforce adoption and data reliability.

Significance of the Topic

Meaningful Salesforce adoption is important to CVRx because reliable and timely CRM data supports opportunity management, customer engagement, and commercial decision-making. Inconsistent use can create incomplete records, reduce leadership visibility, and make it difficult to understand field activities and customer needs. By identifying adoption barriers and evaluating a targeted engagement campaign, this project can help CVRx improve data quality, workflow consistency, and employee engagement. The findings will also help the company determine which adoption strategies should be continued, modified, or expanded to better support its commercial and marketing goals.

Chapter 2: Background and Analytics Objectives

Current Digital Marketing Practices

Brand Persona

CVRx’s digital brand persona combines patient-centered encouragement with clinical credibility. Patient-facing Barostim communications translate a complex neuromodulation therapy into messaging centered on improving quality of life and helping individuals with heart failure return to meaningful everyday activities. Rather than emphasizing technical device specifications alone, the patient-facing brand frequently connects Barostim with themes of independence, activity, and living beyond the limitations associated with heart failure.

The brand takes a more technical and evidence-oriented approach when communicating with healthcare professionals. Professional content emphasizes the mechanism of action, clinical evidence, appropriate patient selection, implantation, reimbursement, and integration of Barostim into heart-failure care. This creates a dual communication approach in which patients receive accessible and benefit-oriented information while healthcare professionals receive more detailed clinical and procedural information. Both approaches remain consistent with CVRx’s broader patient-centered positioning.

Website and Landing Pages

The Barostim website functions as a central component of CVRx’s digital marketing ecosystem. Patient-facing pages explain heart failure, introduce Barostim, describe how the therapy works, present patient experiences, address frequently asked questions, and provide information about the treatment process. The website therefore supports users across multiple stages of the decision journey, ranging from initial awareness to consideration and potential evaluation.

The website also contains action-oriented pathways intended to move interested visitors toward the next stage of the patient journey. A prominent “Do I Qualify?” pathway allows visitors to answer questions related to potential treatment eligibility and provide information for additional support. Other website resources help users learn about treatment locations and connect with healthcare professionals familiar with Barostim.

CVRx maintains separate resources for healthcare professionals. These pages provide more detailed information regarding clinical evidence, patient identification, professional education, reimbursement, implantation, and related clinical resources. The website therefore serves both patient and professional audiences while directing each group toward content appropriate to its role in the treatment process.

Keyword Research and SEO

CVRx’s public website contains content addressing search topics closely related to its target patient population and product category. These topics include heart failure, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), Barostim, Baroreflex Activation Therapy, heart-failure treatment, treatment qualification, symptoms, and device-based treatment options.

Educational pages and frequently asked questions also address informational queries that potential patients or caregivers may encounter while researching heart-failure treatment. This content structure can support organic discovery by matching website information with questions users may enter into search engines.

However, public information does not reveal CVRx’s internal keyword-research methodology, target keyword portfolio, SEO platform, backlink strategy, rankings, organic traffic, or search-performance KPIs. A complete SEO assessment would require access to tools such as Google Search Console, website analytics, or internal SEO reports. Therefore, the public website demonstrates search-relevant content development, but the effectiveness of CVRx’s SEO strategy cannot be determined from publicly available information alone.

Email Marketing

Email and direct digital communication may support continued education after prospective patients express interest in Barostim or submit information through digital channels. These communications can potentially help individuals access educational resources and understand subsequent steps in the treatment journey.

Public information, however, provides limited visibility into CVRx’s current email-marketing infrastructure. The company does not publicly disclose its email service provider, segmentation methodology, automated workflows, campaign frequency, audience size, or performance indicators such as open rates, click-through rates, and downstream conversions.

Consequently, email should be considered a potential component of CVRx’s patient communication and nurturing ecosystem, but its current scale and effectiveness cannot be independently assessed without internal marketing data.

Social Media

Social media provides CVRx with an additional channel for increasing awareness of Barostim and distributing educational content to patients, caregivers, and healthcare professionals. Patient stories, educational information, treatment explanations, company developments, and clinical content can extend the reach of information hosted on the Barostim website.

This channel is particularly relevant because neuromodulation for heart failure may be unfamiliar to potential patients. Social content can help introduce the treatment category before an individual actively searches for Barostim, while patient-centered content can make the therapy easier to understand and connect the technology with real-world experiences.

Evaluating the effectiveness of these activities would require channel-level information such as impressions, reach, engagement, referral traffic, audience characteristics, and conversions. Public-facing social activity can demonstrate that the channel is being used, but it does not provide sufficient evidence to determine its contribution to patient acquisition or treatment adoption.

Paid search could support CVRx’s patient-acquisition strategy by reaching individuals actively searching for information about heart failure, persistent symptoms, treatment alternatives, or device-based therapies. Search advertising could then direct appropriate users toward educational content, qualification resources, or other relevant Barostim webpages.

However, publicly available information does not provide sufficient evidence to determine the current scope of CVRx’s paid-search program. Paid social media could complement CVRx’s direct-to-consumer strategy by generating awareness among patients and caregivers who may not yet be actively searching for Barostim. Educational videos, patient experiences, quality-of-life messaging, and treatment-awareness content could potentially support this objective.

Public information does not provide enough detail to determine the company’s current paid-social platforms, targeting methodology, media spending, campaign structure, or performance. Any assessment of paid-social effectiveness would therefore require internal campaign and analytics data.

Display advertising could theoretically support awareness and remarketing throughout the longer consideration process associated with a specialized medical therapy. However, because Barostim is a regulated medical device and digital advertising may involve health-related information, campaign execution must also account for applicable regulatory, privacy, and advertising-platform requirements.

Without internal advertising data, the role and performance of display advertising within CVRx’s current digital marketing program cannot be reliably assessed.

Conversion Rate Optimization

The Barostim website contains several features consistent with conversion-oriented digital design. Calls to action direct users toward learning whether they may qualify for Barostim, while educational resources address questions that could otherwise prevent users from progressing further in the treatment journey.

The qualification pathway is particularly important because it provides a transition from general education to potential patient identification. Supporting information such as patient experiences, frequently asked questions, treatment explanations, and provider resources may further reduce informational barriers and help visitors determine an appropriate next step.

The presence of these features does not establish that CVRx currently operates a formal conversion-rate-optimization program. Access to website analytics and internal testing information would therefore be necessary to evaluate CRO performance.

Other Digital Marketing Activities

Digital education represents an important component of CVRx’s broader market-development strategy. Online educational resources allow the company to explain HFrEF, introduce Barostim, communicate potential benefits and risks, and provide patients with pathways for learning more about treatment.

CVRx also maintains digital resources for healthcare professionals, including clinical evidence, professional education, patient-identification information, reimbursement resources, and other materials supporting the implementation of Barostim programs.

Together, these activities demonstrate that CVRx’s digital ecosystem extends beyond conventional advertising. Digital channels support patient education, professional education, treatment awareness, potential patient identification, and connection with healthcare providers across multiple stages of the Barostim journey.

Customer Influence Strategy

Demand Generation

CVRx’s demand-generation strategy involves increasing awareness and understanding of Barostim across multiple stakeholder groups, including patients, caregivers, physicians, advanced practice providers, and healthcare organizations. Because Barostim represents a specialized device-based therapy for HFrEF, demand generation requires education not only about the brand but also about the treatment category and the patients for whom the therapy may be appropriate.

Patient-facing digital communication can introduce Barostim to individuals who may be seeking additional options for persistent heart-failure symptoms. Educational website content, patient experiences, digital outreach, and other awareness activities can then direct individuals toward additional information about the therapy.

Professional demand generation operates alongside patient outreach. Physician education, clinical evidence, key opinion leader engagement, and referral-network development can increase awareness among healthcare professionals responsible for identifying, evaluating, referring, and treating appropriate patients.

The strategy therefore reflects the multi-stakeholder nature of medical-device adoption. Generating demand for Barostim requires building awareness among patients while simultaneously developing clinical awareness and confidence among the healthcare professionals and institutions involved in treatment.

Demand Harvesting

CVRx’s patient journey provides several mechanisms for converting existing awareness and interest into further action. Potential patients who encounter Barostim through digital marketing, search, physician referral, educational content, or other sources can use the website to learn more about the therapy and investigate whether they may meet preliminary qualification criteria.

The “Do I Qualify?” pathway represents an important transition from general awareness to potential patient identification. Rather than ending the digital experience with educational content, the qualification process provides interested individuals with a structured next step.

Demand harvesting also occurs through CVRx’s professional and commercial infrastructure. Account Managers develop relationships with cardiology centers and relevant physicians, while Field Clinical Specialists provide clinical and procedural support. Market-access resources can further assist with reimbursement and prior-authorization requirements as appropriate patients progress toward treatment.

The overall customer journey can therefore be conceptualized as:

Awareness → Education → Qualification → Provider Connection → Clinical Evaluation → Authorization → Treatment

This pathway demonstrates how CVRx’s digital, commercial, clinical, and market-access activities can work together to move appropriate potential patients from awareness toward evaluation and treatment.

Loyalty Building

Loyalty within CVRx’s market differs from conventional consumer-product loyalty because Barostim is an implantable medical therapy rather than a frequently repurchased product. Relationship development therefore extends across patients, physicians, referral networks, and healthcare organizations.

For patients, continued education, treatment support, follow-up communication, and access to relevant resources can help maintain confidence throughout the treatment journey. Patient experiences may also contribute to advocacy and awareness by allowing individuals to communicate how treatment has affected their quality of life.

For healthcare professionals and institutions, loyalty is more closely associated with confidence, continued engagement, and integration of Barostim into appropriate treatment pathways. Account Manager relationships, Field Clinical Specialist support, professional education, reimbursement assistance, and consistent clinical support can contribute to stronger long-term relationships with participating centers.

Traditional repeat-purchase metrics alone would therefore provide an incomplete view of loyalty. More relevant measures could include continued physician engagement, referral activity, active treatment-center participation, patient satisfaction, advocacy, follow-up engagement, and sustained utilization among healthcare organizations offering Barostim.

Analytics Infrastructure

CVRx’s analytics environment for this CEP is centered on Salesforce, which serves as an important source of commercial and customer-relationship data. Salesforce can provide behavioral information about how members of the commercial organization document and manage activities, accounts, opportunities, and other relevant workflow processes. For this project, these records provide a potential foundation for measuring meaningful Salesforce adoption rather than relying only on self-reported system usage.

The proposed analytics framework distinguishes between system activity and meaningful use. Relevant Salesforce measures may include record completeness, update timeliness, documented activities, completion of required workflow steps, and other role-appropriate behaviors that can be reliably extracted from the available data. These measures can support the adoption index proposed in AO1 and provide the dependent measures needed to investigate adoption drivers and barriers in AO2.

Salesforce data alone, however, cannot fully explain why users adopt the platform differently. Behavioral records can show what users document in the system but cannot directly measure perceptions such as usefulness, ease of use, training effectiveness, managerial support, administrative burden, or workflow compatibility. Survey responses, interviews, and other approved employee feedback can therefore provide complementary information for interpreting behavioral patterns.

The project’s analytics infrastructure can consequently be viewed as two related evidence streams. The first consists of behavioral Salesforce data describing observable system use. The second consists of employee-level or qualitative information describing users’ experiences with Salesforce. When permitted identifiers and comparable time periods are available, these sources may be analyzed together. If reliable linkage is not possible, the behavioral and perceptual evidence should be analyzed separately and compared at an aggregate level.

Several limitations must be considered. Salesforce activity does not necessarily represent the full range of work completed outside the platform, and a completed field does not automatically indicate that the information entered is accurate or useful. Differences in roles, territories, workloads, account responsibilities, and required workflows may also affect apparent adoption levels. Survey and interview information may introduce self-reporting or response bias, while small sample sizes may limit the statistical methods that can be applied.

The final analytics infrastructure used for the CEP will therefore depend on the data CVRx authorizes the team to access. Before modeling begins, the team should document available Salesforce objects and fields, required workflows, historical coverage, user roles, missing-data patterns, survey variables, and any restrictions on connecting employee-level information. This validation will determine which measures and analytical methods proposed in AO1 through AO4 can be implemented reliably.

Competitive Analysis

Industry Overview

CVRx operates within the cardiovascular medical-device industry and, more specifically, within the market for device-based therapies addressing heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). Heart failure is a chronic cardiovascular condition in which the heart cannot circulate blood effectively enough to meet the body’s needs. Treatment commonly involves guideline-directed medical therapy, while selected patients may also receive implantable device-based therapies.

Barostim occupies a differentiated position within this environment because it uses Baroreflex Activation Therapy to influence the autonomic nervous system rather than directly pacing or stimulating the heart muscle. CVRx describes Barostim as the first and only commercially available neuromodulation device indicated to improve symptoms in patients with HFrEF. The therapy is intended to complement guideline-directed medical therapy for appropriately indicated patients rather than replace pharmaceutical treatment.

Competition within this market extends beyond products with identical mechanisms of action. CVRx competes for physician attention, hospital adoption, reimbursement, clinical acceptance, patient awareness, and commercial resources within the broader heart-failure treatment environment. Important competitive factors include clinical effectiveness, safety, reliability, ease of use, reimbursement, product support, clinical evidence, and sales-force experience.

Industry Size and Outlook

Heart failure represents a large global healthcare burden. CVRx estimates that approximately 64 million people worldwide have heart failure, including approximately 6.7 million people in the United States and 10.5 million across Germany, France, Italy, Spain, and the United Kingdom.

Within its currently addressed HFrEF population, CVRx estimates an annual market opportunity of approximately $2.2 billion in the United States and $2.8 billion across those five European markets. These estimates reflect the population the company believes may be eligible for Barostim under its current commercial strategy and regulatory indications.

The longer-term opportunity could expand if Barostim receives evidence and regulatory support for broader patient populations. CVRx’s BENEFIT-HF clinical program is evaluating Barostim in an expanded heart-failure population. The company has estimated that a successful expansion could increase its prevalence-based U.S. addressable population from approximately 339,000 to approximately 983,000 patients. Because that potential expansion depends on future clinical, regulatory, reimbursement, and commercialization outcomes, it should be considered a prospective opportunity rather than part of the currently addressable market.

The industry’s outlook is also shaped by continued innovation in pharmaceuticals, implantable devices, remote monitoring, and other heart-failure technologies. As new therapies emerge, companies must demonstrate not only clinical effectiveness but also reimbursement viability, physician acceptance, operational support, and the ability to identify appropriate patients.

Key Competitors

CVRx identifies other device-based therapies for HFrEF as an important source of competition. The most directly comparable commercially available device identified in CVRx’s disclosures is Cardiac Contractility Modulation (CCM), delivered through the Optimizer Smart Mini system developed by Impulse Dynamics. CCM is designed for selected symptomatic heart-failure patients who remain symptomatic despite guideline-directed medical therapy and are not indicated for cardiac resynchronization therapy.

Barostim and CCM address overlapping portions of the heart-failure population but use different therapeutic mechanisms. Barostim stimulates baroreceptors in the carotid artery to influence autonomic cardiovascular function, whereas CCM delivers electrical signals to the heart intended to modify cardiac contractility.

CVRx also operates within a broader competitive environment that includes established cardiac-device manufacturers such as Medtronic, Boston Scientific, Abbott, and Johnson & Johnson. These organizations participate in various portions of cardiovascular and heart-failure care and possess substantially greater financial, technical, clinical, sales, and marketing resources than CVRx.

Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) represents another established device-based treatment for certain heart-failure patients. CRT devices coordinate contractions between the heart’s ventricles and are offered by large medical-device manufacturers. Barostim is not simply a replacement for CRT because patient eligibility and mechanisms differ; instead, these therapies exist within a broader treatment landscape in which physicians determine the appropriate therapy according to each patient’s clinical characteristics.

Pharmaceutical therapies are also central to HFrEF treatment. CVRx does not consider existing pharmaceutical therapies direct competitors because Barostim is intended to complement guideline-directed medical therapy. Nevertheless, pharmaceutical innovation can influence treatment pathways, physician decision-making, and the population considered for additional device-based therapies.

Competitor Digital Marketing Strategies

Digital marketing within the heart-failure device market relies heavily on patient education, physician education, clinical evidence, treatment qualification, and provider-location resources. Because these therapies require medical evaluation and physician involvement, competitor websites generally function as educational and patient-navigation platforms rather than conventional e-commerce environments.

Impulse Dynamics provides a useful comparison. Its digital presence for the Optimizer Smart Mini emphasizes the patient-facing message “Hope Is Here,” quality-of-life improvements, patient testimonials, treatment education, and a physician-finder pathway. The company also maintains separate professional content explaining CCM therapy, clinical evidence, patient eligibility, reimbursement, and procedural information.

Large cardiovascular-device companies such as Medtronic similarly maintain extensive patient and healthcare-professional digital ecosystems. Medtronic provides educational pages explaining cardiac resynchronization therapy, available CRT devices, benefits and risks, patient support, and clinical information for healthcare professionals.

CVRx follows a comparable dual-audience structure but differentiates its messaging around Barostim’s neuromodulation mechanism and patient journey. The Barostim website combines patient stories and quality-of-life messaging with a qualification questionnaire and connection to Barostim-trained cardiologists. Healthcare-professional content emphasizes clinical evidence, patient identification, treatment implementation, and reimbursement.

Digital competition therefore extends beyond advertising reach. Companies compete through the clarity of patient education, visibility in treatment-related searches, strength of clinical evidence, physician resources, treatment-center accessibility, patient stories, and the ability to convert digital interest into appropriate clinical evaluation.

Customer Analysis

Customer Segments

CVRx operates in a multi-stakeholder healthcare market in which the purchaser, clinical decision-maker, referral source, payer, and patient may represent different participants. Customer analysis should therefore extend beyond a single definition of the customer.

The first major segment consists of healthcare institutions, particularly hospitals and cardiovascular treatment centers. Hospitals represent an important commercial customer because they purchase Barostim and provide the clinical setting in which implantation occurs. Institutional adoption depends on factors including physician interest, patient volume, reimbursement, clinical support, operational readiness, and the organization’s ability to identify eligible patients.

The second segment consists of healthcare professionals. This includes heart-failure specialists, electrophysiologists, interventional and general cardiologists, advanced practice providers, vascular surgeons, and other clinicians involved in identifying, referring, evaluating, or treating potential Barostim patients. These stakeholders require clinical evidence, patient-selection guidance, procedural education, and treatment-support resources.

The third segment consists of patients with HFrEF who may satisfy the clinical criteria for Barostim. Patients may enter the journey through their physicians, referrals, digital outreach, online research, patient stories, or other awareness channels. Their information needs differ from those of clinicians and include understanding their condition, treatment eligibility, potential benefits and risks, the implant process, insurance considerations, and what to expect after treatment.

A fourth stakeholder group consists of third-party payers. Although payers are not conventional customers, reimbursement and prior-authorization decisions can influence whether eligible patients can progress toward treatment. Market-access activity is therefore an important component of CVRx’s broader commercial strategy.

Segment Analysis

The institutional segment is important because expanding Barostim requires more than generating patient awareness. CVRx must establish and maintain treatment centers capable of identifying, evaluating, implanting, and supporting appropriate patients. Commercial relationships with hospitals and cardiovascular centers therefore represent an important component of market expansion.

Healthcare professionals influence several stages of the patient journey. Referring clinicians must recognize potentially eligible patients, treating cardiologists must evaluate appropriateness, implanting physicians require procedural knowledge, and clinical teams must support patients after implantation. Professional education and clinical evidence are therefore central to this segment.

Patients represent the ultimate beneficiaries of Barostim and an increasingly important audience within CVRx’s direct-to-consumer strategy. The company’s website allows potential patients to complete a short qualification questionnaire and then connect with a Barostim Coordinator and a trained cardiologist. This creates a digital pathway through which patient interest can potentially be translated into clinical evaluation.

These segments are interdependent. Patient awareness alone cannot produce treatment without physician evaluation and institutional capability. Physician interest cannot produce treatment if eligible patients are not identified or reimbursement barriers prevent access. Hospital adoption may also produce limited utilization if referral networks are not sufficiently developed.

CVRx’s commercial challenge is therefore best understood as a coordinated ecosystem rather than a simple business-to-business or business-to-consumer market. Successful commercialization requires alignment among patients, clinicians, hospitals, referral networks, and payers.

SWOT Analysis

Strengths

CVRx’s primary strength is the differentiated nature of Barostim. The therapy uses a distinct neuromodulation mechanism and is the first FDA-approved medical technology using neuromodulation to improve symptoms in patients with heart failure. This differentiation is supported by specialized clinical expertise, an expanding commercial infrastructure, physician education, field clinical support, reimbursement resources, and an established patient-education ecosystem.

Another strength is the company’s focused specialization in Barostim and cardiovascular neuromodulation. This focus allows CVRx to build clinical, commercial, and educational capabilities around a clearly defined therapy and patient population.

Weaknesses

CVRx remains highly dependent on Barostim as its principal commercial product, creating concentration risk if adoption, reimbursement, clinical acceptance, or market growth does not develop as expected.

The company also competes against substantially larger medical-device organizations with greater financial resources, established physician relationships, broader product portfolios, larger sales organizations, and stronger brand recognition.

From the perspective of this CEP, inconsistent meaningful Salesforce adoption could represent an internal commercial limitation if important customer and field activities are not documented completely or consistently. However, the existence and magnitude of this issue must be established through the analyses proposed in AO1 and AO2 rather than assumed.

Opportunities

A major opportunity is continued penetration of the currently addressable HFrEF population. CVRx estimates a multibillion-dollar annual market opportunity in the United States and major European markets, suggesting substantial room for additional patient and treatment-center adoption.

Further opportunities include expanding physician and patient awareness, increasing the number and productivity of treatment centers, strengthening referral networks, improving digital patient identification, and using commercial data more effectively to support customer engagement.

Clinical development may create additional long-term opportunities. The BENEFIT-HF program is evaluating Barostim in a broader heart-failure population, which could substantially expand the addressable market if supported by successful clinical, regulatory, and reimbursement outcomes.

The Salesforce CEP itself represents an operational opportunity. Improved understanding of meaningful adoption, user barriers, user segments, and engagement strategies could help CVRx strengthen the quality and consistency of its commercial information infrastructure.

Threats

CVRx faces competition from established and emerging heart-failure therapies. Competing medical-device companies may introduce technologies that demonstrate stronger clinical outcomes, lower costs, easier implantation, improved patient convenience, or other advantages.

Large cardiovascular-device companies also possess substantially greater financial, research, clinical, sales, and marketing resources. Their established relationships with physicians and hospitals may create competitive pressure as new technologies enter adjacent heart-failure populations.

Reimbursement represents another potential threat because patient access to implantable medical technologies depends partly on coverage and payment policies. Changes in reimbursement, prior-authorization requirements, or payer policies could influence treatment access and hospital adoption.

Other threats include regulatory uncertainty, dependence on continued clinical evidence, technological change, challenges in recruiting and retaining qualified commercial personnel, and the possibility that future pharmaceutical or device-based innovations alter existing heart-failure treatment pathways.

Research Framework and Literature Review

AO1 — Measure Meaningful Salesforce Adoption (Luis Berumen)

Existing Research and Models

Measuring Salesforce adoption starts with deciding what successful use means. A person can open the platform regularly and still leave records incomplete or enter information too late to support a decision. The research reviewed for AO1 offers three useful perspectives: the quality of the information a system produces, the tasks completed through it, and the conditions that help people use it. Together, these perspectives provide a basis for the proposed index, while leaving its specific scoring rules to be developed for CVRx.

The Information Systems Success Model separates system quality, information quality, service quality, use, user satisfaction, and net benefits. These dimensions are related, but they describe different parts of success (DeLone and McLean 2003). For AO1, this distinction means platform activity and record quality should be examined separately before combining them. Required-field completion and update timeliness would describe the information available to CVRx, while documented activities would describe selected uses of Salesforce. A limitation is that a completed field may still contain inaccurate information. The model provides a way to organize the measures, but it does not establish which CVRx fields are necessary or how much weight each measure should receive.

Research on system usage strengthens the case for matching measurement to the work being done. Burton-Jones and Straub define usage through the user, system, and task, and show that the choice of usage measures can change the conclusions drawn about task performance (burtonjones2006?). Task-Technology Fit adds that technology must be used and fit the tasks it supports to contribute to performance (Goodhue and Thompson 1995). Applied to AO1, these frameworks suggest measuring required opportunity updates and workflow steps rather than treating every action as equally useful. They also support checking expectations by role. An action required of one role may be irrelevant to another, so a common activity count could produce an unfair comparison.

Sales technology studies make this measurement issue more concrete. Research distinguishes using technology to access, analyze, and communicate information, showing why sales technology use should be examined through different activities (Hunter and Perreault 2007). In a pharmaceutical sales setting, technology use was associated with improved customer service and adaptability through the mechanisms examined in the study (Ahearne et al. 2008). Together, these studies suggest that the way technology supports sales work matters when interpreting use. For CVRx, I would therefore report the index’s component scores alongside the overall score. These studies concern sales work and performance, however, and do not test a CVRx adoption index. They cannot establish that a higher proposed index score will produce higher sales.

Adoption also continues after a system is introduced. Research on post-adoptive behavior examines how users continue, extend, and deepen their use of installed systems (Jasperson et al. 2005). Evidence from two sales organizations shows that initially positive reactions after training can decline after implementation (Speier and Venkatesh 2002). This combination supports examining patterns across months and collecting feedback rather than relying on a single snapshot. It does not establish that CVRx users have experienced the same problems. Changes in work volume or responsibilities could also affect activity, so those conditions need to be considered when interpreting trends.

Acceptance models help explain the limits of behavioral records. The Technology Acceptance Model identifies perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as important beliefs associated with technology acceptance (davis1989?). UTAUT brings together factors involving expected benefits, effort, social influence, and supporting conditions (venkatesh2003?). Salesforce records cannot directly measure these perceptions. These models support considering user responses when explaining the baseline, but acceptance or an intention to use a system is different from producing complete and timely records. Their survey measures should complement the behavioral measures rather than replace them.

Research on sales force technology also identifies training and organizational support as relevant to use (Buehrer et al. 2005). This provides another reason to avoid interpreting a low score as a lack of willingness. Limited support, a difficult workflow, or unclear expectations could help explain the pattern, but these explanations must be investigated at CVRx. The reviewed studies are older and cover different technologies, tasks, and organizations. Their shared contribution is a reasoned approach to measurement and interpretation. They do not supply current CVRx findings or a ready-made Salesforce scoring formula.

Adequacy of Existing Information

The gathered research is sufficient to support the general direction of AO1: define meaningful use through relevant tasks, examine information quality, show component scores, and consider patterns over time. It is not sufficient to calculate CVRx’s baseline. We still need confirmation of required fields, expected update periods, role-specific workflows, and the available record histories. We also need to know whether the data distinguish missing information from tasks that were not required. Existing research cannot tell us which CVRx workflow has the largest gap or whether a proposed weighting system produces useful comparisons. Those questions require approved company information and a review of the proposed measures with CVRx.

Need for Additional Analysis

Additional analysis is needed to turn the proposed index into a usable baseline. I would first review the approved fields and histories, define eligible records for each measure, and calculate completeness, timeliness, documentation, workflow, and activity scores separately. A month with no relevant opportunities should be identified as having no eligible work for that measure rather than automatically receiving a low score. I would then compare approved roles, territories, and months while considering workload and checking whether alternative weights change the conclusions. Survey or interview responses could help explain whether low scores relate to usefulness, ease, workflow fit, or support. Those responses would address beliefs and experiences that records cannot show. Any connection between survey responses and behavior would depend on approved identifiers and matching time periods; otherwise, the results would be interpreted separately.

Analytics Objective Assessment

I would keep AO1 as written because existing research does not answer the CVRx-specific baseline question. The review refines how I would carry it out: use clearly defined task measures, retain the separate component scores, limit the influence of platform activity, and account for differences in eligible work. I would also keep the distinction between measuring adoption under AO1 and explaining its drivers under AO2. The index would remain a proposed measure until CVRx confirms its rules and the available data support its calculation.

AO2 — Identify Drivers and Barriers of Meaningful Salesforce Adoption (Mickyas Shawel)

Existing Research and Models

After establishing how meaningful Salesforce adoption will be measured in AO1, the next step is to examine the factors associated with differences in adoption across users. Technology-adoption research suggests that system use can be influenced by a combination of individual perceptions, organizational conditions, workflow characteristics, training, managerial support, and experiences with the technology. These factors provide a theoretical foundation for AO2, but they should be treated as potential drivers and barriers rather than assumed explanations for Salesforce adoption at CVRx.

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) provides one framework for understanding individual technology acceptance. Davis identifies perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as important beliefs associated with technology acceptance (davis1989?). Applied to Salesforce, perceived usefulness would describe whether users believe the platform helps them perform their work more effectively, while perceived ease of use would describe the degree to which using the system is perceived as requiring limited effort. These constructs provide potential explanations for differences in Salesforce adoption, but they cannot establish whether usefulness or ease of use currently explains adoption patterns at CVRx without company-specific evidence.

The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) expands this perspective by incorporating performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions (venkatesh2003?). This framework is relevant because Salesforce adoption may depend on more than an individual’s opinion of the platform. Expectations from managers and coworkers, availability of organizational support, training, and access to necessary resources may also influence whether employees incorporate Salesforce into their normal work routines. For AO2, UTAUT therefore provides a basis for examining both individual perceptions and organizational conditions associated with meaningful adoption.

Task-Technology Fit provides another perspective by focusing on the relationship between technology and the work users are expected to perform. Goodhue and Thompson argue that technology is more likely to contribute to individual performance when its capabilities fit the tasks being performed (Goodhue and Thompson 1995). In the CVRx context, this suggests examining whether Salesforce supports or complicates the workflows of commercial users. Workflow compatibility may function as a potential driver of adoption, while redundant processes, unnecessary administrative requirements, or poor alignment between Salesforce and established work routines may represent potential barriers. However, these relationships must be evaluated using CVRx-specific information rather than inferred from the framework alone.

Research focused specifically on sales technologies provides additional support for examining these factors. Avlonitis and Panagopoulos studied CRM technology acceptance among salespeople and found that perceived usefulness was an important influence on acceptance, along with accurate expectations regarding system usage, salesperson innovativeness, perceived ease of use, and supervisor encouragement and support (Avlonitis and Panagopoulos 2005). This research is particularly relevant to AO2 because it examines CRM acceptance within a sales-force environment rather than general technology adoption. Nevertheless, the study involved a different organization and CRM environment, so its findings identify candidate factors for CVRx rather than establishing which factors currently influence Salesforce adoption within the company.

Research on sales force technology also highlights the importance of organizational support and training. Buehrer and colleagues examined reasons for using sales force technology, barriers to its use, and organizational support surrounding adoption (Buehrer et al. 2005). Their findings provide a basis for examining factors such as training, management support, technical support, and perceived productivity benefits. For CVRx, these factors could help determine whether differences in meaningful Salesforce adoption are associated with users’ experiences with the system and the organizational environment surrounding its use.

Job experience may also contribute to differences in technology perceptions. Guan and colleagues examined sales force automation acceptance across different levels of sales experience and found differences in areas such as perceived productivity and efficiency gains, perceptions of sales force automation as a micromanagement tool, and satisfaction with system functionality (Guan et al. 2004). This suggests that employee characteristics and professional experience may be relevant when interpreting Salesforce adoption patterns. AO2 should therefore avoid assuming that all users experience Salesforce in the same way.

Adoption should also be viewed as an ongoing process rather than a one-time decision. Jasperson and colleagues conceptualize post-adoptive behavior as the ways individuals continue, extend, and modify their use of information systems after implementation (Jasperson et al. 2005). Evidence from sales organizations similarly indicates that initially favorable reactions toward sales force automation may change after employees begin using the technology in their actual work environment (Speier and Venkatesh 2002). These findings support examining current Salesforce experiences rather than assuming that initial training or acceptance reflects sustained meaningful use.

Research on sales technology further suggests that technology use can involve multiple work activities rather than a single measure of system activity. Hunter and Perreault examine how sales technology can support accessing, analyzing, and communicating information (Hunter and Perreault 2007). Research in a pharmaceutical sales environment also links salesperson technology use with mechanisms involving customer service and adaptability (Ahearne et al. 2008). These studies reinforce the importance of examining Salesforce within the context of commercial work. They do not establish that greater Salesforce use will automatically improve performance at CVRx, but they support investigating whether users perceive the system as valuable to their work and whether those perceptions are associated with the meaningful-adoption measures established in AO1.

Together, the literature identifies several theoretically supported candidate drivers of meaningful Salesforce adoption, including perceived usefulness, ease of use, managerial support, training effectiveness, workflow compatibility, appropriate expectations, and perceived value of CRM information. Potential barriers include administrative burden, system complexity, insufficient support, redundant processes, perceptions of excessive monitoring, and poor alignment with established sales routines. AO2 will use these factors as a framework for investigation rather than treating them as existing findings about CVRx.

Adequacy of Existing Information

The existing literature provides sufficient theoretical support for identifying potential drivers and barriers of Salesforce adoption, but it cannot determine which factors are most relevant within CVRx. The reviewed studies were conducted in different organizations, industries, technology environments, and time periods. Their findings provide constructs that can guide the analysis, but they cannot establish the relative importance of those constructs for CVRx employees.

Additional company-specific information is therefore required. AO2 will depend on the meaningful-adoption measures established in AO1 as well as approved information describing employees’ experiences with Salesforce. Relevant information may include survey responses, interviews, training experiences, workflow characteristics, job or role information, managerial support, perceived usefulness, ease of use, and other organizational or engagement variables that CVRx makes available.

It will also be important to distinguish between behavioral Salesforce records and employee perceptions. A user could perceive Salesforce as useful while still maintaining incomplete or untimely records. Conversely, another user could maintain high-quality records while reporting frustration with the platform. Behavioral measures and employee perceptions therefore provide different forms of evidence and should not automatically be treated as interchangeable measures of adoption.

The available information must also be evaluated for completeness and comparability. If survey, interview, or employee information cannot be reliably connected with the adoption measures developed in AO1, those sources may need to be analyzed separately rather than combined at the individual-user level. Similarly, differences in roles, territories, workloads, or Salesforce requirements may need to be considered before interpreting differences between users.

Need for Additional Analysis

Additional analysis is required to determine which candidate factors are actually associated with meaningful Salesforce adoption at CVRx. The analysis should begin by examining the distribution and quality of the available driver and barrier measures. Descriptive statistics and group comparisons can then be used to identify patterns in adoption across relevant employee characteristics, roles, territories, training experiences, or other approved groups.

Correlation analysis may provide an initial assessment of relationships between potential explanatory variables and the meaningful-adoption measures established in AO1. For example, the analysis could examine whether higher perceived usefulness, stronger managerial support, greater training effectiveness, or better workflow compatibility is associated with stronger meaningful-adoption scores.

If the available sample size and measurement quality are sufficient, multivariable regression or classification methods could then be used to evaluate several factors simultaneously. This would allow AO2 to estimate the direction and magnitude of relationships while accounting for other relevant variables. For example, the analysis could examine whether perceived usefulness remains associated with meaningful adoption after considering training, managerial support, job experience, workflow compatibility, or other available characteristics.

Qualitative information from interviews or open-ended survey responses may provide additional context for interpreting quantitative patterns. Users may identify workflow problems, administrative burdens, training gaps, unclear expectations, or useful Salesforce capabilities that are not observable in system records. Combining these perspectives could provide a more complete explanation of the barriers and drivers associated with adoption.

The analysis should avoid interpreting statistical association as proof of causation. Salesforce records and cross-sectional survey responses may reveal meaningful relationships without establishing that one factor caused another. The objective is therefore to identify and quantify factors associated with meaningful adoption and use those findings to guide subsequent analysis rather than to make unsupported causal claims.

Analytics Objective Assessment

AO2 should remain Identify Drivers and Barriers of Meaningful Salesforce Adoption because the existing literature provides theoretical explanations for technology adoption but does not answer the CVRx-specific question. The literature review strengthens the objective by identifying theoretically supported constructs that can be evaluated using company data instead of assuming in advance why employees use Salesforce differently.

The objective should focus on identifying and quantifying associations between potential explanatory factors and the meaningful-adoption measures developed in AO1. Perceived usefulness, ease of use, managerial support, training effectiveness, workflow compatibility, administrative burden, job experience, and related factors should be treated as candidate variables whose relevance must be evaluated using CVRx data. The final analytical methods will depend on the quality, structure, and quantity of the information made available for the project.

AO2 also provides an important connection between the group’s analytics objectives. AO1 establishes how meaningful Salesforce adoption will be measured. AO2 examines factors associated with variation in that adoption. The resulting evidence can then help inform the user segments developed in AO3 and provide a stronger analytical basis for the targeted engagement strategies evaluated in AO4.

The progression of the analytics framework can therefore be summarized as:

AO3 — Salesforce User Segmentation and Targeted Engagement

AO3: Analyze Salesforce usage patterns to identify distinct user segments based on adoption behavior and determine how those segments can inform targeted engagement strategies.

R5: Which distinct user segments exist based on usage patterns, and do the segments differ in their drivers and barriers?

Existence of Well-Established Models

Research on CRM and sales force technologies indicates that technology adoption and usage can vary substantially among users. Studies of sales technology acceptance have identified perceived usefulness, ease of use, job experience, organizational support, training, and other individual or organizational characteristics as factors associated with technology adoption and usage (Avlonitis and Panagopoulos 2005; Guan et al. 2004; Buehrer et al. 2005). These findings suggest that Salesforce users should not necessarily be treated as a homogeneous group when evaluating adoption and designing engagement strategies.

Behavioral segmentation provides one approach for identifying meaningful differences among technology users. Miller et al. (2022) demonstrated that observed digital usage patterns can be used to identify distinct behavioral user segments. Applied to CVRx, relevant behavioral measures may include opportunity-update timeliness, record completeness, documentation of customer interactions, workflow consistency, platform activity, and other approved measures developed through AO1. These measures are consistent with the behavioral components currently proposed for R5 in the project’s Construct and Measurement Table.

Several analytical approaches could be considered for transforming these behavioral differences into user segments. A rule-based or adoption-maturity approach would classify users according to predefined indicators and cutoff values, such as relatively lower, moderate, or higher levels of meaningful adoption. This approach would be transparent and easy to communicate to decision-makers, but the selection of thresholds may be subjective and may oversimplify differences among users.

Behavioral clustering provides a more empirical alternative. Rather than defining groups in advance, clustering uses multiple behavioral variables to identify users with similar patterns. Techniques such as k-means or hierarchical clustering could be considered depending on the size and structure of the available data. Cluster analysis can reveal patterns that predefined categories may miss, but results can depend on decisions involving variable selection, standardization, clustering procedure, and validation. Methodological research therefore emphasizes careful application and evaluation of cluster-based segmentation (Tuma et al. 2011). Segment stability should also be evaluated rather than assumed (Müller and Hamm 2014).

Behavioral patterns alone, however, may not explain why users differ. Technology-adoption research identifies factors such as performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, perceived usefulness, training, organizational support, workflow characteristics, and other contextual experiences as potential influences on technology adoption (Venkatesh et al. 2003; Avlonitis and Panagopoulos 2005; Buehrer et al. 2005). Two users displaying similar Salesforce activity could therefore have different motivations, barriers, or support needs underlying that behavior.

A hybrid behavioral and adoption-driver framework is consequently the preliminary preferred approach for AO3. Under this framework, behavioral Salesforce measures from AO1 would first be used to identify candidate user groups through a transparent rule-based approach or, if the sample size and data quality permit, empirical clustering. Adoption barriers, drivers, needs, and contextual characteristics examined through AO2 could then be used to profile and interpret those groups. Research combining technology-acceptance factors with segmentation provides support for using adoption characteristics to understand heterogeneous user groups (Joshi and Chawla 2023).

This hybrid approach is preferred because it connects observed behavior with potential explanations for that behavior and creates a clearer path from segmentation to managerial action. The final segmentation method should therefore not be selected solely because it produces statistical separation. Candidate segments should also be evaluated for stability, interpretability, sufficient size, and practical usefulness before being used to develop differentiated engagement strategies. The specific segmentation technique should remain flexible until the CVRx sample size, available variables, and data quality are fully evaluated.

Adequateness of Gathered Information

The information gathered so far provides a defensible foundation for AO3 but is not sufficient to determine which user segments actually exist within CVRx. Existing research supports the use of behavioral measures to identify differences among technology users and identifies adoption barriers, drivers, and contextual characteristics that may help explain those differences. The project’s current measurement plan also identifies potential behavioral segmentation inputs from Salesforce and survey measures of perceived usefulness, ease of use, workflow compatibility, manager support, training effectiveness, trust in Salesforce data, administrative burden, and other barriers. However, the team still needs confirmation of the number of Salesforce users available for analysis, the completeness and comparability of the behavioral measures, and whether relevant AO2 survey information can be reliably linked with Salesforce data at the appropriate level. Existing literature can guide the framework and variable selection, but it cannot determine the CVRx-specific answer to R5.

Need for Additional Analysis

Additional analysis is required to determine whether meaningful and actionable Salesforce user segments exist within CVRx. The team should first evaluate the number of users available for segmentation and examine the quality, distribution, and comparability of candidate behavioral variables. Relevant measures should then be selected and standardized where appropriate. A transparent rule-based segmentation can be compared with empirical clustering if the sample size and data structure support it. Any clustering solution should be evaluated for quality and stability rather than accepted solely because an algorithm produces groups. The resulting segments should then be profiled using relevant barriers, drivers, needs, and contextual characteristics from AO2. Differences in workload, territory, opportunity volume, role, or other contextual factors should also be considered before behavioral differences are interpreted as differences in adoption. Finally, each retained segment should be evaluated for interpretability and practical usefulness and translated into an appropriate targeted engagement strategy.

Assessment and Affirmation of Analytics Objectives

AO3 remains affirmed because existing research supports meaningful heterogeneity in technology adoption while leaving the CVRx-specific segmentation question unresolved. The literature indicates that observed behavioral patterns can provide a basis for identifying user groups, while technology-adoption factors can provide additional context for understanding why those groups may differ. This directly supports R5 and PO3 by providing a pathway from Salesforce usage patterns to differentiated engagement strategies.

The preliminary preferred framework is a hybrid behavioral and adoption-driver segmentation approach. Behavioral measures developed through AO1 will provide the initial basis for identifying candidate segments, while barriers, drivers, needs, and contextual characteristics examined through AO2 will help profile and interpret those groups. The exact segmentation technique should remain flexible until the available sample size, behavioral variables, survey measures, and data quality are evaluated. If the data support stable empirical clustering, clustering may be used to identify candidate groups. If they do not, a transparent and defensible rule-based segmentation approach can provide a practical alternative. In either case, segments should be retained only when they are sufficiently stable, interpretable, and actionable to support targeted engagement under PO3 and the subsequent intervention evaluated in AO4.

AO4 — Implement and evaluate an engagement campaign

Existing Research and Models

Evaluating the impact of field engagement interventions requires measuring behavioral change, system adoption, and perceived utility over time (Rodriguez and Boyer 2020). Research on change management and sales enablement suggests that targeted interventions—such as role-specific communications, workflow demonstrations, and manager toolkits—are most effective when aligned with specific user barriers (Abdillah 2025; singh2019?). Speier and Venkatesh (2002) highlight that implementing sales force automation (SFA) technologies often reveals hidden adoption minefields, where early user resistance or perceived friction can negatively impact long-term technology integration (Speier and Venkatesh 2002). In the age of digital transformation, sales professionals must adapt to increasingly complex tools; however, merely adopting SFA tools is insufficient for driving performance unless engagement strategies address individual, organizational, and contextual factors (jelinek2006?; jelinek2013?; singh2019?). Evaluation frameworks must therefore extend beyond raw usage volume, incorporating pre- and post-implementation comparisons of record completeness, data quality, and workflow adherence (Rodriguez and Boyer 2020; jelinek2006?). Quasi-experimental designs that compare intervention groups with reference groups provide a robust method for isolating campaign effects and avoiding the “all pain, no gain” pitfall within active commercial environments (Abdillah 2025; jelinek2013?).

Adequacy of Existing Information

The gathered literature and change-management frameworks are sufficient to define the general evaluation approach for A04: identifying hidden adoption barriers (Speier and Venkatesh 2002), comparing pre- and post-intervention behavioral metrics, measuring shifts in data quality, and contextualizing findings across user segments (singh2019?; jelinek2006?). However, existing research is not sufficient to determine the specific intervention tactics, campaign timeline, or target user groups for CVRx. Those choices depend directly on the specific adoption barriers and user segments identified in A01 through A03, as well as operational feasibility within CVRx’s commercial team.

Need for Additional Analysis

Additional analysis is needed to operationalize and evaluate the engagement campaign. Prior to implementation, baseline metrics for targeted user segments must be established, and intervention activities must be mapped to identified barriers (Speier and Venkatesh 2002). Following campaign execution, comparative analysis must be conducted to measure changes in meaningful adoption, data reliability, and workflow consistency (jelinek2013?). Where possible, differences between the intervention group and reference groups should be evaluated to confirm whether observed improvements can be attributed to the campaign rather than external factors.

Analytics Objective Assessment

I would keep A04 as written because evaluating engagement interventions is critical for translating diagnostic findings into actionable operational improvements. The literature review refines how A04 should be executed: addressing adoption minefields (Speier and Venkatesh 2002), focusing on multi-dimensional evaluation (data quality, workflow adherence, and user perception), controlling for role differences (singh2019?), and using reference group comparisons where operationally feasible to validate campaign effectiveness (jelinek2013?).

Method

Survey data will be analyzed using descriptive statistics and group comparisons to examine differences in Salesforce adoption perceptions, barriers, and engagement needs among CVRx commercial field users. The analysis will build on the adoption-related constructs examined in AO2, including perceived usefulness, ease of use, workflow compatibility, manager support, training effectiveness, trust in Salesforce data, and administrative burden.

Survey responses will first be summarized using frequencies, percentages, means, and measures of variation, as appropriate for each variable. These summaries will help identify common experiences and potential differences across respondents. Where sample size permits, comparisons may be conducted across relevant employee characteristics, such as job role, tenure, or territory.

If CVRx approves the use of consistent de-identified identifiers, survey responses may also be linked to Salesforce adoption measures developed under AO1 and the behavioral user segments identified through AO3. This would allow the analysis to examine whether users within different behavioral segments report different adoption barriers, perceptions, or support needs.

If individual-level linkage is not permitted, survey responses will be analyzed separately and interpreted at an aggregate level. The final analytical procedures will depend on survey participation, available variables, and CVRx’s data-sharing approvals.

Justification

This approach supports AO3 by providing additional context for understanding differences in Salesforce adoption behavior. Although Salesforce records can reveal how frequently users complete required activities or maintain customer information, these records cannot directly explain employees’ perceptions or experiences with the platform.

Survey analysis can help identify whether different user groups experience challenges related to training, administrative workload, managerial support, or perceived usefulness. For example, users with similar adoption patterns may report different reasons for using Salesforce consistently or inconsistently.

Examining these differences will help CVRx develop engagement recommendations that address the needs of specific user segments rather than applying identical communications or training to all employees.

The proposed approach is consistent with the hybrid behavioral and adoption-driver segmentation framework established in Chapter 2. Behavioral measures will provide the primary basis for identifying segments, while survey findings will help interpret those groups and determine which engagement strategies may be appropriate.

The resulting recommendations will also support AO4 by informing the selection of engagement tactics for the proposed intervention.

Variables

The primary survey variables will include perceived usefulness, ease of use, workflow compatibility, manager support, training effectiveness, trust in Salesforce data, and administrative burden. These constructs will be measured using relevant survey questions developed under AO2.

Additional variables may include employee role, tenure, territory, training experience, and other approved characteristics relevant to Salesforce adoption.

Where individual-level data linkage is approved, the Salesforce Adoption Index developed under AO1 and behavioral segment membership established under AO3 may be incorporated into group comparisons.

The final selection of variables will depend on the approved survey instrument, response rates, and the completeness and quality of the collected data.

AO4 — Implement and Evaluate an Engagement Campaign (Wenting Jiang)

Method Used

Survey responses collected before and after the targeted engagement intervention will be compared to evaluate changes in users’ Salesforce perceptions and experiences. Multi-item constructs will be created by averaging related five-point Likert-scale items. When the final sample size is sufficient, Cronbach’s alpha will be used to examine the internal consistency of each construct before a composite score is calculated.

The analysis will begin with descriptive statistics, including response counts, means, standard deviations and response distributions. If the same respondents can be confidentially matched across the two periods, paired-samples tests will be used. If individual responses cannot be matched, baseline and follow-up responses will be treated as independent samples. Nonparametric alternatives will be considered when the sample size or data distribution does not support a t-test.

If an intervention group and a credible comparison group are available, a difference-in-differences analysis will be considered. Open-ended responses will also be reviewed to identify recurring barriers, recommendations and user concerns.

Justification for the Method

A pre/post survey design is appropriate because AO4 examines whether the intervention changes users’ perceptions of Salesforce, the support they receive and their willingness to follow recommended Salesforce practices. Previous research indicates that technology adoption may be influenced by perceived usefulness, ease of use, training, managerial support and workflow compatibility.

Survey results will be interpreted together with Salesforce behavioral data because self-reported responses may be affected by recall error, social-desirability bias and common-method bias. Combining survey and behavioral data will provide a more complete evaluation of the intervention.

Variables

The primary survey outcomes will include perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, workflow compatibility, information quality, managerial support, training adequacy, administrative burden and overall satisfaction. Self-reported update frequency and timeliness may also be examined.

The main independent variables will be the measurement period, intervention-group status and, when applicable, the interaction between intervention-group status and the post-intervention period. Role, tenure, prior CRM experience, region and comfort with learning new software may be considered as contextual variables if the final sample size permits.

AO3 — Salesforce User Segmentation and Targeted Engagement (Tim Arana)

Method

AO3 will use a hybrid behavioral segmentation approach to identify groups of CVRx Salesforce users with similar adoption patterns. The analysis will primarily use approved, de-identified Salesforce CRM records and the meaningful adoption measures developed under AO1. The objective is to determine whether distinct groups of users demonstrate different patterns of Salesforce engagement and whether those differences can inform targeted support strategies.

The analysis will begin by examining the distribution, completeness, and comparability of relevant Salesforce activity measures. Descriptive statistics will be used to summarize adoption behaviors across users and identify potential differences in record completeness, update timeliness, interaction documentation, workflow consistency, and platform activity. Differences in role-specific responsibilities and workloads will be considered to avoid treating lower activity as evidence of weaker adoption when fewer activities are required.

If the available sample size and data quality support statistical clustering, hierarchical clustering or K-means clustering may be explored. Variables will be standardized where appropriate to prevent differences in measurement scales from disproportionately influencing the results. Candidate clustering solutions will be evaluated for stability, interpretability, and practical usefulness.

If clustering is not appropriate due to limited sample size, missing information, or insufficient variation, rule-based segmentation will be used instead. This method will classify users according to documented adoption patterns and clearly defined thresholds. The resulting groups will then be profiled using approved organizational characteristics and relevant findings from AO2.

Justification

Hybrid behavioral segmentation is appropriate for AO3 because Salesforce users may demonstrate different adoption behaviors and experience different challenges when using the platform. Identifying these differences can help CVRx move beyond a single engagement strategy for its entire commercial field organization.

Cluster analysis may reveal behavioral patterns that would not be apparent through predefined categories. However, statistical clustering may produce unstable or difficult-to-interpret groups when the dataset is small or contains limited behavioral variation. Rule-based segmentation provides a more transparent alternative that can be implemented consistently when clustering is not suitable.

This approach is consistent with the research framework established in Chapter 2, which emphasizes combining observed technology usage with adoption-related factors to better understand differences among users.

The resulting segments will help CVRx identify groups that may benefit from different forms of training, communication, managerial support, or workflow assistance. These recommendations will inform the targeted engagement intervention developed and evaluated under AO4.

Variables

The primary segmentation variables will include opportunity update timeliness, record completeness, customer interaction documentation, workflow consistency, and platform activity. These measures will be derived from the approved Salesforce records and the adoption measurement framework developed under AO1.

The overall Salesforce Adoption Index may also be examined when interpreting the resulting segments. However, overlapping measures will be evaluated to avoid assigning excessive influence to the same adoption characteristics during clustering.

Additional profiling variables may include employee role, team, territory, tenure, and other approved organizational characteristics. Where authorized, survey measures from AO2 describing perceived usefulness, training effectiveness, manager support, workflow compatibility, and administrative burden may also be incorporated to interpret differences between segments.

The final selection of variables and segmentation methods will depend on the quality, completeness, and availability of the approved Salesforce dataset.

AO4 — Implement and Evaluate an Engagement Campaign (Wenting Jiang)

Method Used

AO4 will use a longitudinal pre/post design to evaluate changes in Salesforce adoption, data quality and workflow behavior after the targeted engagement intervention. The preferred analytical method will depend on whether a credible comparison group is available.

If both an intervention group and a comparison group are available, difference-in-differences analysis will compare the baseline-to-follow-up change in the intervention group with the corresponding change in the comparison group. The interaction between intervention-group status and the post-intervention period will represent the primary estimate of intervention effectiveness.

If a comparison group is unavailable, a single-group pre/post analysis will be used. When the same users appear in both periods, paired-samples tests or their nonparametric alternatives will compare user-level outcomes. The analysis will report baseline and follow-up values, absolute and percentage changes, effect sizes and confidence intervals.

Regression models may be used to control for relevant differences in role, region, workload, opportunity volume and baseline adoption. The final model type will depend on whether the outcome is continuous, proportional, binary or a count.

Justification for the Method

A longitudinal design is appropriate because AO4 seeks to determine whether Salesforce behavior changes following the intervention. Existing research suggests that training, user support and workflow integration may influence sales-technology adoption and that adoption patterns can change over time.

A comparison group and difference-in-differences design are preferred because a simple pre/post change could result from seasonality, policy changes, workload changes or other organizational events. If no comparison group is available, the results will be described as changes observed after the intervention rather than definitive causal effects.

Salesforce adoption will not be evaluated through login frequency alone. The analysis will emphasize task-relevant measures such as record completeness, update timeliness, customer-interaction documentation and workflow adherence. Survey results from Section 3.1.7 will be used to help explain why behavioral changes did or did not occur.

Variables

The principal outcomes will include record completeness, update timeliness, workflow adherence, customer-interaction documentation and platform activity. A meaningful-adoption composite may also be considered if its scoring rules can be justified.

The primary explanatory variables will be intervention-group status, baseline versus post-intervention period, the Intervention × Post interaction and intervention exposure. Potential control variables will include role, region, tenure, workload, opportunity volume and baseline adoption. All final definitions and analytical periods will be confirmed after CVRx provides the approved data.
