Chapter 1: Introduction

Background and Business Problem(s)

[Describe CVRx, the relevant business environment, and the primary business or marketing problem addressed by the project.]

Project Objectives

The project objectives describe what CVRx wants to accomplish with the team, rather than a statistical technique. Based on the faculty project briefing and kickoff discussion with Professor Moore, the four objectives are:

  1. Measure current Salesforce adoption. Establish a baseline for meaningful use and data reliability across approved field workflows.
  2. Identify adoption barriers and drivers. Understand what helps or prevents field users from keeping Salesforce information current and useful.
  3. Segment users and develop a targeted engagement strategy. Group users by relevant patterns and design support or communications that address their needs.
  4. Implement and evaluate an engagement campaign. Pilot a limited intervention and assess changes in use, data quality, and user feedback.

Analytics Objectives

AO1 — Measure Meaningful Salesforce Adoption (Luis Berumen)

My analytical objective is to develop a Salesforce Adoption Index and use it to examine how adoption differs across approved roles, territories, and time periods. The index would bring together opportunity update timeliness, record completeness, documentation of customer interactions, consistency with defined workflows, and a smaller measure of platform activity. I would first confirm with CVRx which fields and actions are actually required for its commercial decisions. Then I would use approved Salesforce records to calculate separate scores for each area and look at the overall pattern. The unit of analysis could be a deidentified user-month, with results shared in aggregate.

This objective answers PO1 by showing where use is consistent and where records are late or incomplete. It could help CVRx decide which workflow needs attention first and provide a baseline for evaluating the later pilot. I would compare like with like: a user with few active opportunities should not receive a low score simply because there was less work to enter. Research supports matching usage measures to the task rather than relying on time spent or access alone (burtonjones2006?). Sales technology can also affect work in different ways, so an overall score should be accompanied by the separate measures and context (ahearne2008?). This is a proposed measure; its final rules and weights depend on the data CVRx approves and the field team’s actual workflow.

AO2 – Identify Drivers and Barriers of Meaningful Salesforce Adoption

Lead: Mickyas Shawel

Identify and quantify the factors associated with meaningful Salesforce adoption among CVRx sales representatives, with particular attention to the behavioral, organizational, and user-experience factors that may encourage or inhibit consistent CRM use.

This analytics objective builds on AO1 by moving beyond the measurement of Salesforce adoption to investigate why adoption levels differ across users. The analysis will examine available Salesforce usage measures alongside relevant employee, workflow, training, and engagement variables to identify factors associated with stronger or weaker adoption. Potential drivers may include perceived usefulness, ease of use, managerial support, training effectiveness, workflow compatibility, and the perceived value of CRM information, while barriers may include administrative burden, system complexity, redundant processes, insufficient training, or limited integration with established sales routines.

The analysis will use the variables available in the final dataset to evaluate relationships between potential adoption factors and the meaningful-adoption measures established in AO1. Depending on the measurement structure and data quality, appropriate methods may include descriptive comparisons, correlation analysis, group comparisons, and multivariable regression or classification models. The purpose is not only to determine whether a factor is associated with adoption, but also to estimate the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of those relationships while accounting for other relevant variables.

The findings from AO2 will provide an evidence-based explanation of the factors most closely associated with Salesforce adoption and will directly support subsequent analytics objectives. In particular, the identified drivers and barriers can help inform the user segments developed in AO3 and provide a stronger analytical basis for designing targeted engagement strategies rather than applying the same intervention to all Salesforce users.

AO3 — Salesforce User Segmentation and Targeted Engagement

Lead: Tim Arana

Analyze Salesforce usage patterns to identify distinct user segments based on adoption behavior and determine how those segments can inform targeted engagement strategies.

This analytics objective supports PO3 by examining differences in Salesforce adoption behavior among CVRx sales representatives and identifying meaningful groups of users with similar usage patterns. Prior research shows that CRM technology acceptance among salespeople can vary based on individual and organizational factors (Avlonitis and Panagopoulos 2005). Research on sales force automation also found differences in technology acceptance associated with salespeople’s job experience (Guan et al. 2004). The analysis will consider relevant Salesforce usage and activity measures available from the client and may incorporate findings from the analyses of current adoption levels and adoption barriers and drivers. Identifying these user segments will allow the team to evaluate whether different users require different engagement approaches rather than applying a single strategy across the entire sales force. The findings will provide an analytical foundation for developing targeted engagement strategies and for the subsequent implementation and evaluation of the engagement campaign.

AO4 — Implement and evaluate an engagement campaign.

Lead: Wenting Jiang

This analytics objective is to evaluate the effectiveness of a targeted field engagement intervention designed to improve meaningful Salesforce adoption among CVRx’s commercial field users. The intervention may include role-specific communications, short workflow demonstrations, training sessions, manager toolkits, or other engagement tactics selected based on the adoption barriers and user segments identified in the earlier objectives. The evaluation will compare meaningful Salesforce adoption, data quality, workflow consistency, and user perceptions before and after implementation. When operationally feasible, the analysis will also compare the intervention group with a reference group.

This objective supports PO4 by implementing an engagement strategy, measuring its outcomes, and identifying what CVRx should continue, modify, or expand. Evaluation measures will be aligned with employees’ actual work requirements rather than relying only on login frequency or time spent in the platform(Rodriguez and Boyer 2020). Because sales technology can affect employees differently depending on their roles and working conditions, the results will also be interpreted in the context of the selected user segment and intervention design (Abdillah 2025). The findings will help CVRx determine whether the campaign produces meaningful and measurable improvements in Salesforce adoption and data reliability.

Significance of the Topic

Meaningful Salesforce adoption is important to CVRx because reliable and timely CRM data supports opportunity management, customer engagement, and commercial decision-making. Inconsistent use can create incomplete records, reduce leadership visibility, and make it difficult to understand field activities and customer needs. By identifying adoption barriers and evaluating a targeted engagement campaign, this project can help CVRx improve data quality, workflow consistency, and employee engagement. The findings will also help the company determine which adoption strategies should be continued, modified, or expanded to better support its commercial and marketing goals.

Research Framework and Literature Review

AO1 — Measure Meaningful Salesforce Adoption (Luis Berumen)

Existing Research and Models

Measuring Salesforce adoption starts with deciding what successful use means. A person can open the platform regularly and still leave records incomplete or enter information too late to support a decision. The research reviewed for AO1 offers three useful perspectives: the quality of the information a system produces, the tasks completed through it, and the conditions that help people use it. Together, these perspectives provide a basis for the proposed index, while leaving its specific scoring rules to be developed for CVRx.

The Information Systems Success Model separates system quality, information quality, service quality, use, user satisfaction, and net benefits. These dimensions are related, but they describe different parts of success (delone2003?). For AO1, this distinction means platform activity and record quality should be examined separately before combining them. Required-field completion and update timeliness would describe the information available to CVRx, while documented activities would describe selected uses of Salesforce. A limitation is that a completed field may still contain inaccurate information. The model provides a way to organize the measures, but it does not establish which CVRx fields are necessary or how much weight each measure should receive.

Research on system usage strengthens the case for matching measurement to the work being done. Burton-Jones and Straub define usage through the user, system, and task, and show that the choice of usage measures can change the conclusions drawn about task performance (burtonjones2006?). Task-Technology Fit adds that technology must be used and fit the tasks it supports to contribute to performance (goodhue1995?). Applied to AO1, these frameworks suggest measuring required opportunity updates and workflow steps rather than treating every action as equally useful. They also support checking expectations by role. An action required of one role may be irrelevant to another, so a common activity count could produce an unfair comparison.

Sales technology studies make this measurement issue more concrete. Research distinguishes using technology to access, analyze, and communicate information, showing why sales technology use should be examined through different activities (hunter2007?). In a pharmaceutical sales setting, technology use was associated with improved customer service and adaptability through the mechanisms examined in the study (ahearne2008?). Together, these studies suggest that the way technology supports sales work matters when interpreting use. For CVRx, I would therefore report the index’s component scores alongside the overall score. These studies concern sales work and performance, however, and do not test a CVRx adoption index. They cannot establish that a higher proposed index score will produce higher sales.

Adoption also continues after a system is introduced. Research on post-adoptive behavior examines how users continue, extend, and deepen their use of installed systems (jasperson2005?). Evidence from two sales organizations shows that initially positive reactions after training can decline after implementation (speier2002?). This combination supports examining patterns across months and collecting feedback rather than relying on a single snapshot. It does not establish that CVRx users have experienced the same problems. Changes in work volume or responsibilities could also affect activity, so those conditions need to be considered when interpreting trends.

Acceptance models help explain the limits of behavioral records. The Technology Acceptance Model identifies perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as important beliefs associated with technology acceptance (davis1989?). UTAUT brings together factors involving expected benefits, effort, social influence, and supporting conditions (venkatesh2003?). Salesforce records cannot directly measure these perceptions. These models support considering user responses when explaining the baseline, but acceptance or an intention to use a system is different from producing complete and timely records. Their survey measures should complement the behavioral measures rather than replace them.

Research on sales force technology also identifies training and organizational support as relevant to use (buehrer2005?). This provides another reason to avoid interpreting a low score as a lack of willingness. Limited support, a difficult workflow, or unclear expectations could help explain the pattern, but these explanations must be investigated at CVRx. The reviewed studies are older and cover different technologies, tasks, and organizations. Their shared contribution is a reasoned approach to measurement and interpretation. They do not supply current CVRx findings or a ready-made Salesforce scoring formula.

Adequacy of Existing Information

The gathered research is sufficient to support the general direction of AO1: define meaningful use through relevant tasks, examine information quality, show component scores, and consider patterns over time. It is not sufficient to calculate CVRx’s baseline. We still need confirmation of required fields, expected update periods, role-specific workflows, and the available record histories. We also need to know whether the data distinguish missing information from tasks that were not required. Existing research cannot tell us which CVRx workflow has the largest gap or whether a proposed weighting system produces useful comparisons. Those questions require approved company information and a review of the proposed measures with CVRx.

Need for Additional Analysis

Additional analysis is needed to turn the proposed index into a usable baseline. I would first review the approved fields and histories, define eligible records for each measure, and calculate completeness, timeliness, documentation, workflow, and activity scores separately. A month with no relevant opportunities should be identified as having no eligible work for that measure rather than automatically receiving a low score. I would then compare approved roles, territories, and months while considering workload and checking whether alternative weights change the conclusions. Survey or interview responses could help explain whether low scores relate to usefulness, ease, workflow fit, or support. Those responses would address beliefs and experiences that records cannot show. Any connection between survey responses and behavior would depend on approved identifiers and matching time periods; otherwise, the results would be interpreted separately.

Analytics Objective Assessment

I would keep AO1 as written because existing research does not answer the CVRx-specific baseline question. The review refines how I would carry it out: use clearly defined task measures, retain the separate component scores, limit the influence of platform activity, and account for differences in eligible work. I would also keep the distinction between measuring adoption under AO1 and explaining its drivers under AO2. The index would remain a proposed measure until CVRx confirms its rules and the available data support its calculation.

AO3 — Salesforce User Segmentation and Targeted Engagement

Existing Research and Models

Research on CRM and sales force technologies indicates that adoption and usage can vary substantially among users. Studies of sales technology acceptance have identified perceived usefulness, ease of use, job experience, organizational support, training, and other individual or organizational characteristics as factors associated with technology adoption and usage (Avlonitis and Panagopoulos 2005; Guan et al. 2004; Buehrer, Senecal, and Bolman Pullins 2005). These findings suggest that a sales force should not necessarily be treated as a homogeneous group when evaluating CRM adoption.

Behavioral segmentation provides one approach for identifying meaningful differences among technology users. Behavioral segmentation provides one approach for identifying meaningful differences among technology users. Miller et al. (2022) demonstrated that observed digital usage patterns could be used to identify distinct user segments, while Namin et al. (2026) found behavioral and psychographic characteristics useful for explaining differences in technology acceptance. For CVRx, similar behavioral indicators could potentially include Salesforce activity frequency, timeliness of opportunity updates, record completeness, consistency of system use, and other approved measures of engagement.

However, behavioral activity alone may not explain why users exhibit different adoption patterns. Technology acceptance research emphasizes factors such as performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, task complexity, role perceptions, and organizational experiences (Speier and Venkatesh 2002; Venkatesh et al. 2003; Rangarajan, Jones, and Chin 2005). Therefore, two sales representatives displaying similar Salesforce activity could have different motivations or barriers underlying that behavior. Research also cautions that cluster-derived segments should not automatically be assumed to be stable or meaningful over time (Müller and Hamm 2014). A hybrid approach that combines segmentation with technology-adoption factors may therefore provide more interpretable and actionable user groups (Joshi and Chawla 2023). #### Adequacy of Existing Information

Existing research provides a strong foundation for understanding technology adoption, user heterogeneity, and potential approaches to segmentation. The literature identifies behavioral, perceptual, organizational, and contextual factors that may distinguish technology users and provides evidence that usage patterns can support meaningful segmentation. However, the existing research is not sufficient to determine which segments exist within CVRx specifically. Prior studies were conducted in different organizations, industries, technologies, and user populations, and several were not focused on CRM systems or medical-device sales organizations. Therefore, the literature can guide the selection of variables and analytical approaches, but it cannot substitute for analysis of CVRx’s own Salesforce users and organizational context.

Need for Additional Analysis

Additional analysis is needed to determine whether meaningful Salesforce user segments exist within CVRx and whether those segments can support differentiated engagement strategies. CVRx-specific Salesforce usage data should be examined to identify patterns in adoption behavior, while findings from the analysis of adoption barriers and drivers may provide additional context for interpreting those patterns. Relevant measures may include activity frequency, timeliness of updates, record completeness, consistency of system use, and other indicators available in the approved dataset. User or organizational characteristics may also be considered where appropriate. Because differences in workload, territory, opportunity volume, or job responsibilities could influence observed Salesforce activity, these factors should be evaluated before interpreting behavioral differences as differences in adoption.

Analytics Objective Assessment

Based on the literature reviewed, AO3 remains appropriate for supporting PO3 but should use a flexible analytical approach. Existing research supports examining Salesforce usage patterns to identify meaningful differences among users, while also showing that behavioral data alone may not fully explain technology adoption. Therefore, the analysis should initially identify candidate segments from available Salesforce usage measures and then incorporate relevant adoption barriers, drivers, and contextual characteristics when interpreting those groups. Any resulting segments should also be evaluated for stability, interpretability, and practical usefulness before they are used to develop targeted engagement strategies. The specific segmentation technique should remain flexible until the CVRx dataset, available variables, and sample size are fully evaluated.
